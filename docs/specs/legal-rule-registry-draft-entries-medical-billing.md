## ENTRY 1 — CPRC §41.0105 / Haygood v. De Escabedo: paid or incurred

**Cite:** Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code §41.0105; Haygood v. De Escabedo, 356 S.W.3d 390 (Tex. 2011).

**(a) Core rule.** [READ — Haygood] Recovery of past medical expenses is limited to amounts "actually paid or incurred by or on behalf of the claimant" — expenses that have been or will be paid — excluding the difference between that amount and charges the provider bills but has no legal right to be paid. Haygood, 356 S.W.3d at 396–97. The statute operates "in addition to any other limitation under law." The rule prevents a windfall to the claimant (following Daughters of Charity, 226 S.W.3d 409 (Tex. 2007)).

**(b) Evidentiary bar.** [READ — Haygood; Henderson] The limitation governs the evidence at trial, not merely recovery: "only evidence of recoverable medical expenses is admissible at trial." Haygood at 399. Mechanics: charges the provider cannot collect are irrelevant to damages (TRE 402), and any residual relevance to noneconomic damages is substantially outweighed by likely confusion (TRE 403, Haygood at 398 & n.57). Post-verdict adjustment does not cure erroneous admission of unadjusted bills; the resulting verdict rests on legally insufficient evidence and requires new trial. Henderson v. Spann, 367 S.W.3d 301, 304–05 (Tex. App.—Amarillo 2012, pet. denied). Collateral source rule survives for the recoverable amounts: jury is not told about insurance coverage or that adjustments were insurance-driven. Haygood at 399–400.

**(c) Uninsured / LOP / factoring branch — full liability means full amount incurred.** [READ — Katy Springs; Primoris; and see Gallegos as cited therein] Where the claimant remains legally liable for the full billed amount — no adjustment has occurred and no contract prohibits the provider (or its assignee/factor) from collecting in full — the full billed amount is "incurred" and admissible; the discounted price a factoring company paid for the receivable is not the measure. Katy Springs & Mfg. v. Favalora, 476 S.W.3d 579, 601–03 (Tex. App.—Houston [14th Dist.] 2015, pet. denied); Primoris Energy Servs. Corp. v. Myers, 569 S.W.3d 745, 762–63 (Tex. App.—Houston [1st Dist.] 2018) (op. on reh'g; note: parties settled, original opinion withdrawn and this opinion issued in its stead — flag posture). An assignee stands in the assignor-provider's shoes.

**(c-1) TIMING.** [READ — Ahmed] "Incurred" is measured by the provider's legal right to be paid as of trial. A post-verdict, prejudgment lien reduction is not a debt reduction (debt merely undersecured) and does not reduce the judgment; applying it post-verdict would be the procedure Haygood rejected. Ahmed v. Sosa, 514 S.W.3d 894, 896 (Tex. App.—Fort Worth 2017).

**(c-2) STALE/UNPAID BILLS.** [READ — Sheppard] A hypothetical, waivable defense the patient could assert against the provider's collection claim (e.g., limitations on 4-year-old unpaid bills) does not convert into a §41.0105 evidentiary bar against the patient's tort recovery. Forth's limitations sentence is dictum. Court expressly protective of LOP-based treatment. Sheppard v. Martinez, No. 14-23-00473-CV, 2025 Tex. App. LEXIS 1266, at *17–22 (Tex. App.—Houston [14th Dist.] Feb. 27, 2025, mem. op.) [CONFIRM subsequent history — none shown in copy on file].

**(c-3) QUALIFIED-LOP CAVEAT — ATTORNEY RULING NEEDED.** [READ — K&L; JUDGMENT] In re K&L Auto Crushers: where the LOPs promised payment "only for any reasonable and necessary medical charges," the Court found nothing legally obligating the claimant to pay unreasonable amounts — and held that even if he were so obligated, tortfeasor liability is independently capped at a reasonable amount. 627 S.W.3d 239, 250–51 (Tex. 2021). Implication for our Kostura qualified-LOP template (which contains the same reasonableness condition): the client is arguably not "obligated" for amounts above reasonable, which (i) protects the client and the lien-clearance analysis, but (ii) gives the defense a K&L-grounded argument that billed amounts above reasonable were never "incurred" at all. **Michael to rule on how the registry states the incurred amount under a qualified LOP.** The billing module must not hard-code either answer meanwhile.

**(d) Scope limit.** [READ — Huntress] The Haygood framework does not govern a plaintiff's no-evidence summary-judgment burden when suing the provider itself for the provider's own tort; incurring any expense caused by the wrongful conduct raises a fact issue without proof of reasonableness/necessity or paid-vs-incurred amounts. Huntress v. Hickory Trail Hosp., L.P., No. 05-19-00892-CV, 2020 Tex. App. LEXIS 4112, at *19–21 (Tex. App.—Dallas May 29, 2020, pet. denied) (mem. op.).

**(e) Currency.** [NEG] No post-2011 Texas Supreme Court decision or 2025–2026 legislation altering the core holding or the statute's text. Consistent with everything in the collection through Jan. 2026, but requires citator pass on Haygood + current statute pull.

**Dropped from external draft:** McMillan v. Hearne, 584 S.W.3d 505 (Tex. App.—Texarkana 2019) was cited by the external response for (a) and (c). [READ] It is a nonsubscriber-employer offset case under Labor Code §406.033 (post-verdict equitable offset for plan payments allowed; no offset for bill-negotiation vendor fees; Haygood distinguished as inapposite). It discusses Haygood accurately but does not apply the paid-or-incurred measure and involves no LOP. Keep only, if at all, as: "discussing Haygood in the nonsubscriber offset context."

- *In re Allstate Indem. Co.*, 622 S.W.3d 870 (Tex. 2021), **docket No. 20-0071 confirmed on the caption** `[READ 2026-07-26]` — the two-*Allstate* identity question is CLOSED; Kostura's "In re Allstate" and this opinion are the same case.

**Propositions:**

**(a) Function of the procedure.** `[READ — Ortiz]` §18.001 is a pretrial mechanism letting a claimant prove reasonableness and necessity of medical expenses by provider/recordkeeper affidavit with itemized statement, avoiding expert testimony at trial. An uncontroverted affidavit is **sufficient but not conclusive** evidence; the defense may still contest reasonableness/necessity at trial through evidence and argument.

**(b) Partial controversion operates CLAIM-BY-CLAIM, not affidavit-by-affidavit.** `[READ — Ortiz, the holding]` A compliant counteraffidavit that controverts only some matters renders the initial affidavit insufficient and inadmissible **only as to the controverted matters**. The uncontroverted portions remain admissible and sufficient. A partially controverted affidavit may not be excluded in its entirety solely because portions are controverted. (The Dallas court's contrary affidavit-level reading — and the dissent's — is rejected 7–1.)

**(c) Claimant's three options on receipt of a partial counteraffidavit.** `[READ — Ortiz]` (1) opt out of §18.001 entirely and prove everything by expert testimony; (2) hybrid — rely on §18.001 for the uncontroverted claims and put on expert testimony for the controverted ones; (3) abandon the controverted claims. If proceeding on the affidavit, the claimant **must redact** the controverted services/charges and the affidavit statements about their reasonableness/necessity — those portions are hearsay again.

**(d) Expert testimony still required for properly controverted claims.** `[READ — Ortiz; READ — Chefs' Produce]` A properly controverted claim cannot be proven by affidavit alone. See (g) for the limitation on how far *Chefs' Produce* carries this.

**(e) The counteraffidavit is itself admissible — including when offered BY the claimant.** `[READ — Ortiz]` Where the counteraffiant affirmatively attests to what portion of a charge IS reasonable, the counteraffidavit is admissible to support those amounts; §18.001 itself supplies the hearsay pathway (no need for the party-admission exclusion). The statute says "party," not "defendant," when addressing who offers a counteraffidavit — confirmed on the face of (e), (e-1), and (g), each of which speaks of "the party offering the counteraffidavit." Practical effect: a partial counteraffidavit is a concession exhibit the claimant can use to prove the conceded floor.

**(f) The uncontroverted floor is a damages ceiling absent experts.** `[READ — Ortiz]` If the claimant relies on the affidavits/counteraffidavits without expert testimony, the jury may award **at most the total uncontroverted amount**; any award above it is unsupported by legally sufficient evidence and remittitur is appropriate. In *Ortiz* this was determinate per line: $2,950 (Synergy, per Le Cara) and $9,774.54 (LifeSciences, per Guitreau's per-charge reasonable amounts).

**(g) *Chefs' Produce* limited by *Ortiz* n.5.** `[READ — both]` *Chefs' Produce* has two holdings: (i) the trial court abused its discretion by striking a medical expert's counteraffidavit and testimony on reasonableness and necessity, because the counteraffidavit gave reasonable notice; and (ii) the counteraffidavit's inclusion of a causation opinion was **not** a proper basis for striking the whole counteraffidavit — conditional mandamus was appropriate because the order effectively foreclosed the opposing party from presenting expert rebuttal on reasonableness and necessity. Its "may not reach the jury… without expert testimony" language at 301 must be read against that actual issue. Record *Chefs' Produce* as **limited-by-*Ortiz*** on this point, not as a standalone affidavit-level rule.

**(h) Necessity and reasonableness controvert independently, per item.** `[READ — Ortiz]` A counteraffidavit may challenge necessity of a service, reasonableness of a charge, or both, item by item. In *Ortiz*: Le Cara challenged necessity of some Synergy services only; Guitreau challenged reasonableness of all six LifeSciences charges but necessity of none — so the LifeSciences affidavit stayed admissible as to necessity throughout. Per-line status is therefore **two flags, not one** (necessity-controverted / reasonableness-controverted).

**(i) Statutory redaction hook.** `[READ — Ortiz, citing §18.002(b-2)]` A charge in an attached bill that is not recoverable is itself inadmissible by reference — the statute anticipates partial presentation of itemized statements.

**(j) The pre-§18.001 expert-requirement statement is contested inside the Court.** `[READ — Ortiz dissent n.1; JUDGMENT on weight]` *Allstate*'s statement (622 S.W.3d at 876) that reasonableness could historically be proven **only** by expert testimony is attacked by Justice Sullivan as probably dicta and probably wrong, with a floated jury-trial constitutional concern. One justice, in dissent, in a footnote — no precedential force, but do not encode "expert testimony always required absent §18.001" as settled. **Live exhibit for the H77 `precedential_status` schema question.**

> **Reading note added 2026-07-26.** Michael's read of *Allstate* located the opinion's centre of gravity in the §18.001(f) qualification and reasonable-notice analysis — see (m) and (n). He did **not** locate the p.876 expert-testimony statement or the p.882 abuse-of-discretion-for-excluding-evidence statement as load-bearing to the holding. That is consistent with, but does not by itself establish, the dicta characterization in (j). Carried as open item **V1**.

**(k) Error-preservation trap.** `[READ — Ortiz n.1]` Ortiz waived her expert-designation issue by offering the supplemental disclosures only in an offer of proof after the close of evidence, without obtaining a ruling before the parties rested. Offer **and obtain the ruling** before resting.

**(l) Service deadlines — now stated from official statutory text.** `[STATUTE — pulled 2026-07-26]`

| Instrument | Deadline | Prongs |
|---|---|---|
| Affidavit — §18.001(d) | **earlier of** | (1) 90 days after the date the defendant files an answer; (2) the date the offering party must designate any expert witness under a court order; (3) the date the offering party must designate any expert witness as required by the TRCP |
| Affidavit, provider first treating **after** the answer — §18.001(d-1) | **earlier of** | (1) court-ordered expert designation date; (2) TRCP expert designation date — *the 90-day prong drops out entirely* |
| Counteraffidavit — §18.001(e) | **earlier of** | (1) 120 days after the date the defendant files its answer; (2) court-ordered expert designation date; (3) TRCP expert designation date |
| Counteraffidavit responding to a (d-1) affidavit — §18.001(e-1) | **LATER of** | (1) 30 days after service of the affidavit; (2) court-ordered expert designation date; (3) TRCP expert designation date |

**(m) Counteraffiant qualification — §18.001(f).** `[STATUTE; READ — Allstate]` The counteraffidavit must be made by a person qualified **by knowledge, skill, experience, training, education, or other expertise** to testify in contravention of all or part of any of the matters in the initial affidavit. *Allstate* is the qualification case: the plaintiff struck the counteraffiant as unqualified, the trial court agreed, and the Supreme Court held the counteraffiant **was** qualified.

**(n) Reasonable notice — content standard, and its separation from reliability.** `[READ — Allstate]` Two propositions:

- **(n-1) What satisfies reasonable notice.** The counteraffidavit must give reasonable notice of the basis on which the serving party intends at trial to controvert the claim reflected by the initial affidavit. In *Allstate* the counteraffidavit "unquestionably" satisfied that requirement because it **itemized each charge being controverted as unreasonable** and **compared the charges in the initial affidavits against median charges for the same services, in the same timeframe, in the same ZIP code**. That is a usable content template, not merely a standard.
- **(n-2) Reasonable notice ≠ reliability.** Determining whether a counteraffidavit meets §18.001(f)'s reasonable-notice standard **does not require the court to assess the reliability of the expert's opinions** under Rule 702 or *Robinson*. The trial court in *Allstate* struck the counteraffiant as unreliable; the Supreme Court held that reasoning flawed. Reliability is not litigated at the counteraffidavit stage.

**(o) Causation may not be controverted by counteraffidavit.** `[STATUTE — §18.001(f), final sentence]` The counteraffidavit may not be used to controvert the causation element of the cause of action. This is the statutory hook that locks with (g): the prohibition exists, and the remedy for violating it is excision of the causation opinion, not destruction of the counteraffidavit.

**(p) Clerk-notice filing obligations — two of them.** `[STATUTE]` §18.001(d-2): the party offering the **affidavit** must file notice with the clerk when serving it (and, except as provided by the Rules of Evidence, the affidavit itself need not be filed with the clerk before trial commences). §18.001(g): the party offering the **counteraffidavit** must file written notice with the clerk when serving it. Two discrete, provable, checkable steps — not judgment calls.

**Structural findings for the build `[JUDGMENT — proposed, unruled]`:**

1. **§18.001 deadlines are DERIVED, not standalone.** Two of the three prongs in both (d) and (e) are expert-designation dates. TRCP 195.2 can therefore pull the §18.001 deadline well inside the 90/120-day figures. The affidavit clock is a downstream consumer of the deadline skeleton, not an independent timer. Any implementation that hardcodes 90/120 days is wrong on a large share of cases.
2. **(e-1) is the only "later of" in the section.** A computed-deadline function that assumes "earlier of" throughout will silently produce a wrong date on precisely the hardest fact pattern to notice — the provider who first treats after the answer. Needs its own fixture and test case.
3. **"the date the defendant files an answer" is singular and undefined for multi-defendant staggered answers.** This is the *same* ambiguity already open against the TRCP 194.2(a) first-answer-or-appearance anchor. One ruling covers both; do not resolve them separately. Carried as open item **V2**.

## ENTRY 3 — Negotiated-rate & cost discovery: North Cypress / K&L line

**Cite:** In re N. Cypress Med. Ctr. Operating Co., 559 S.W.3d 128 (Tex. 2018) (substituted opinion — project PDF is the original 2018 Tex. LEXIS 346 slip; use 559 S.W.3d cite); In re K&L Auto Crushers, LLC, 627 S.W.3d 239 (Tex. 2021) (reh'g denied Sept. 3, 2021).

**(a) Core discovery rule.** [READ — North Cypress; K&L] When reasonableness of charges is contested, the following are relevant (not dispositive) and discoverable: the provider's negotiated reimbursement rates with private insurers; Medicare/Medicaid reimbursement rates for the services at issue; the provider's Medicare cost reports; the provider's costs for the services/devices billed; and chargemaster methodology. North Cypress (hospital-lien reasonableness, uninsured patient); K&L at 249–51 (extended to personal-injury suits: "The reasonableness of the claimant's medical expenses is as germane in a personal-injury case as it is in a suit to challenge the validity of a medical lien").

**(b) Doctrinal basis.** [READ — K&L] §41.0105's "in addition to any other limitation" preserves the independent common-law requirement that recoverable medical expenses be reasonable (Dallas Ry. v. Gossett, 294 S.W.2d 377 (Tex. 1956), and 19th-century line). Charged amount alone is not evidence of reasonableness. K&L at 249–50.

**(c) Reaches LOP providers; reduced non-party protection.** [READ — K&L] The discovery reaches Letter-of-Protection providers on the plaintiff's side; LOP providers have a direct financial stake in the case outcome and "forfeit a degree" of the protection afforded disinterested non-parties. K&L at 253–54.

**(d) Confidentiality → protective order, not denial.** [READ — North Cypress; K&L] Trade-secret/confidentiality objections are addressed by protective order; on a TUTSA trade-secret assertion, Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code §134A.006(a) creates a presumption in favor of a protective order. Complete denial without considering that alternative is abuse of discretion. K&L at 255–56.

**(e) Proportionality; tailoring.** [READ — K&L; Central Oregon] Requests must be tailored to the same/similar services and devices at issue as of treatment dates; K&L-style narrowed requests approved; but relevance does not entitle a party to all tangentially related documents, and trial courts must run the Rule 192.4 balancing. In re Cent. Or. Truck Co., 644 S.W.3d 668, 669–70 (Tex. 2022) (per curiam) (near-total denial of billing discovery — expressly including adjustments, payments, payor identity, insurer contracts, and the LOPs themselves — was abuse of discretion; remanded for tailoring under K&L; also approving discovery of pre-accident medical/employment/insurance records on causation and earning capacity).

**(f) LIMIT — no counterfactual insurer discovery (Fourth Court).** [READ — In re United Healthcare] K&L does not authorize deposing the plaintiff's health insurer about the rates it would have paid on claims never submitted. Discovery targets the treating providers' actual rates; hypothetical reimbursement on unsubmitted claims is irrelevant. In re United Healthcare Ins. Co., 652 S.W.3d 458, 461–62 (Tex. App.—San Antonio 2022, orig. proceeding). (Defense had pleaded failure-to-mitigate for not using health insurance; court did not reach that theory's merits.) **This is our home appellate district.**

**(g) Extension trend — UCR/surprise-billing litigation.** [READ — Christus] Federal court (E.D. Tex., Sherman Div. — note: not W.D.) extends the relevance logic to compel discovery of hospital costs/billing methodology in Ch. 1467 "usual and customary rate" litigation, while acknowledging "reasonable" and "usual and customary" are distinct questions. Christus Santa Rosa Health Care Corp. v. UnitedHealthcare Ins. Co., 2026 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 353 (E.D. Tex. Jan. 5, 2026). Persuasive only; shows direction of travel.

**(h) Admissibility side (pre-K&L).** [READ — Primoris] Excluding a hospital billing director's testimony of ~400% markups and a pre-negotiated 40% factoring rate was harmful error — relevant to reasonableness even though the full billed amount was "incurred" and admissible. Primoris at 763–64. The two threads (full amount in; reasonableness attackable) operate simultaneously.

**(i) Currency.** [NEG] No post-2021 SCOTX narrowing. Consistent with the collection (Central Oregon extends; United Healthcare is an intermediate-court boundary, not a narrowing of the core rule); requires citator pass.

## ENTRY 4 — Prop. Code Ch. 55 hospital/EMS lien caps & perfection

**Status:** DRAFT — primary text read 2026-07-26 (full chapter pulled); wording sign-off pending; entry status NOT verified.

**(a) EMS county threshold — RESOLVED.** [STATUTE — §55.002(c), pulled 2026-07-26] A county with a population of **800,000 or less** — a **ceiling, not a floor**; EMS provider liens exist only in the *smaller* counties. Practice consequence, and it cuts both ways across the practice: **Bexar County is over the threshold, so no EMS lien attaches on a San Antonio case; Uvalde County is far under it, so EMS liens do attach there.** A county-conditional rule the module can **compute** from the case's county rather than prompt for. `[JUDGMENT]` Population figures change with the census; the threshold check needs a data source and a re-check cadence, not a hardcoded county list.

**(b) The fatal-defect conflict — the statute sorts more cleanly than the draft assumed.** `[JUDGMENT on the classification; STATUTE for each item]`

Goes to whether the lien exists at all (**validity**):
- 72-hour admission window — §55.002(a); 72-hour EMS-receipt window — §55.002(c)
- EMS provider in a county over 800,000 — §55.002(c)
- filing written notice of lien with the county clerk **of the county in which the services were provided**, **before money is paid** to an entitled person — §55.005(a)(2)
- the §55.003(b) non-attachment categories: workers' compensation, FELA, LHWCA; and proceeds of an insurance policy in favour of the injured individual or their beneficiary/legal representative, **except** public liability insurance carried by the insured
- §55.003(c): no hospital lien against a railroad owner/operator that maintains the hospital treating the individual

Goes only to **amount** (reduction, not death) — all of §55.004:
- first 100 days of hospitalization; 50% of all amounts recovered; or the trier-of-fact-specified hospital-charge amount less the pro rata share of reasonable attorney's fees and expenses — §55.004(b), lesser of
- physician emergency-care charges, first seven days only — §55.004(c)
- charges exceeding a reasonable and regular rate — §55.004(d)(1), (g)(1)
- charges where the physician or EMS provider accepted insurance/indemnity benefits, **regardless of whether those benefits equalled the full charge** — §55.004(d)(2), (d)(3), (g)(2), (g)(3)
- physician who is a member of the legislature — §55.004(d)(4)
- **charges barred under CPRC §146.003 — §55.004(d)(5)** (see (f); cross-ref Entry 5)
- EMS cap of **$1,000**, for services in the 72 hours following the accident — §55.004(f)

Schema consequence `[JUDGMENT — proposed, unruled]`: lien validity is a **boolean with a reason code**; amount reduction is a **separate computed field**. Two workflows, not one. A defective lien and an over-stated lien are different objects and the disbursement checklist treats them differently.

**(c) What the statute does NOT resolve — the notice-defect question stays open.** [STATUTE] §55.005(g) is the only savings provision, and it is narrow: the **failure of an individual to receive** a notice mailed in accordance with (d) does not affect the lien's validity. It says nothing about a failure to **send** at all, or about a defective EMS authorization-form notice under (e) (bolded, ≥14-point, separate paragraph, signed unless §773.008 applies). The negative implication cuts toward those being fatal, but that is inference, not text. **If the Entry 4 conflict is specifically about notice defects, the statute cannot close it and case law must.** Carried as open item **V4** — still gates the disbursement checklist. (The prior draft's misnaming/substantial-compliance sub-question rides with V4.)

**(d) Four findings not previously in the draft.** [STATUTE]
1. **§55.0015 (added 2019, H.B. 2929) — "admitted" is broad.** An injured individual is considered admitted if allowed access to **any department** of the hospital for **any** treatment, care, or service. ER-only visits count. This resolves in the hospital's favour a question that used to be litigated.
2. **§55.001(4) + §55.002(a) — arguably no hospital lien where the tortfeasor is a county or school district.** "Person" is defined to exclude a county, common, or independent school district, and §55.002(a) requires an accident attributed to the negligence of **another person**. Read straight, that is categorical. `[JUDGMENT — Claude's reading, explicitly unverified.]` Carried as open item **V5**.
3. **§55.007 is the disbursement checklist's actual teeth.** A release of a cause of action or judgment to which a Ch. 55 lien may attach is **not valid** unless (1) the lien charges were paid in full before execution and delivery of the release, (2) they were paid to the extent of any full and true consideration paid to the injured individual, or (3) the lienholder is a party to the release. Getting this wrong does not merely leave a lien outstanding — **it clouds the release itself**.
4. **§55.008 — records access.** On an attorney's request, the hospital or EMS provider must make records available as promptly as possible, may issue reasonable access rules, but **may not deny access because a record is incomplete**. The records are admissible subject to the rules of evidence. Useful lever; worth a practice hook.

**(e) Sequencing trap for the deadline logic.** [STATUTE — §55.005(d)] The five-business-day notice clock does **not** run from the filing of the lien. It runs from the date the hospital or EMS provider **receives notice from the county clerk** that the notice of lien has been recorded. Any date function anchored on the filing date is wrong.

The timely-billing analysis in **Entry 5** feeds directly into the Ch. 55 lien-amount calculation here; any implementation that computes them in isolation will over-state liens. ## ENTRY 5 — CPRC Ch. 146 (timely billing): bar, lien interaction, H.B. 4145

**Status:** DRAFT — primary text read 2026-07-26 (enrolled H.B. 4145 read in full); wording sign-off pending; entry status NOT verified.

**Cite:** Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code ch. 146 (NOT Insurance Code — spec correction); H.B. 4145, 89th Leg., R.S., ch. 1092 (eff. Sept. 1, 2025).

**(a) What the statute actually is.** [STATUTE] A timely-billing statute, not an EOB-based lien ceiling. §146.002 deadlines: bill the patient/responsible person by the first day of the 11th month after services (a); plan issuers and government payors per contract/regulation or the same 11-month default ((b)–(c)). §146.003 bar: a violating provider "may not recover from the patient" amounts the patient would have been entitled to receive as plan payment/reimbursement or would not otherwise have owed had the provider billed timely; bar extends to family members otherwise responsible. **Spec's prior framing ("EOB patient-responsibility amount is the maximum permissible lien") is wrong as a description of Ch. 146** — EOB-as-ceiling concepts live in the surprise-billing statutes (Ins. Code Ch. 1467 / SB 1264 world), per external draft [EXT]. Spec-feedback item filed.

**(b) H.B. 4145 amendment.** [STATUTE — enrolled bill read in full 2026-07-26] Sole change: new §146.002(c-1) — where a provider furnishes services to a patient related to a personal injury claim for which the patient is represented by an attorney, and **unless otherwise directed by the patient or other responsible person**, the provider *may* satisfy §146.002(a) by submitting the bill **to the patient's attorney** by the first day of the 11th month after the date of service; conforming change to §146.002(d)(1) — "attorney representing the patient" **inserted** into the list of permissible recipients, at the address shown on the provider's records. Effective Sept. 1, 2025.

**(b-1) Applicability — RESOLVED, and the per-bill flag SURVIVES.** [STATUTE — enrolled bill] SECTION 2: the change in law applies **only to a health care service provided on or after the effective date**; a service provided before that date is governed by prior law, continued in effect for that purpose. SECTION 3: effective **September 1, 2025**. Keyed to the **date of service**, not the date of billing — the analysis is genuinely **per bill, not per case**: a single treatment course spanning last September has bills on both sides of the line, each governed by different law. **The per-bill exposure flag in (d) is confirmed as real** and should proceed to wording sign-off.

**(b-2) The lever.** `[JUDGMENT — proposed, unruled]` The negative implication: if billing the attorney had already satisfied §146.002, the Legislature had no reason to add (c-1). So for **pre-9/1/2025 services**, a provider who billed only counsel and never the patient has a compliance problem; §146.003 bars recovery of those charges; and per §55.004(d)(5) those charges also drop out of the hospital lien. **Three provisions chained.**

**(b-3) Two qualifications the module must carry.** [STATUTE; JUDGMENT as noted]
1. **(c-1) is permissive and overridable.** "May satisfy" — the attorney route is an option the provider elects, not a substituted obligation; and the patient or other responsible person can direct otherwise, taking the route off the table for that provider. Whether directing otherwise is ever tactically worth it is a real question, since it means the client starts receiving the bills. `[JUDGMENT — flagged, not ruled]`
2. **§146.002(d)(2) is a complete answer in many cases.** Mailing or otherwise submitting the bill to the issuer of the health benefit plan or third-party payor, as required by that plan or payor, **is** billing. A provider who timely submitted to the carrier has complied even if nothing ever reached counsel or the client. The module should surface this **before** anyone relies on a §146.003 argument.

**(b-4) Computation trap.** [STATUTE] The deadline is **not** "11 months." It is **the first day of the 11th month after the date the services are provided**. A March 2026 service is due by **February 1, 2027** — roughly ten and a half months. Off-by-one is easy here and the entire recovery bar depends on it. **Wants its own tested function and fixture.**

**(c) Interaction with Haygood/§41.0105 — ATTORNEY RULING NEEDED.** [READ — Sheppard; JUDGMENT] Sheppard rejected converting a *waivable* limitations defense into a §41.0105 exclusion. §146.003 is materially different: a self-executing statutory bar ("may not recover"), plus §55.004(d)(5) carve-out from hospital liens [EXT]. A statutory bar fits Haygood's "no legal right to be paid" test far better than the argument Sheppard rejected — but **no court in our collection has held §146.003 operates as a Haygood exclusion.** Treat as open. Registry status: unverified proposition, argument-grade only.

**(d) Practical exposure window.** [JUDGMENT, derived] For services billed only to plaintiff's counsel (not the patient) *before* Sept. 1, 2025, a §146.002(a) violation argument exists once the 11-month mark passes; (c-1) closes this prospectively. Billing module: per-bill flag = (service date + billing route + 11-month clock + pre/post-9/1/2025). Estimates-not-adjudication disclaimer applies; flag is an issue-spotter only.

**(e) Coverage.** [STATUTE] "Health benefit plan" defined broadly: insurance policies, HMO evidences of coverage, MEWAs, workers' comp policies, and MVA policies to the extent of PIP/med-pay. §146.001(1). "Health care service provider" = state-licensed provider whose services may be paid under such a plan. §146.001(2). No disciplinary action for violations (§146.004).

**(f) Cross-link — Entry 5 and Entry 4 are NOT independent.** [STATUTE — §55.004(d)(5), pulled 2026-07-26] Charges barred under §146.003 drop out of the Ch. 55 hospital lien per §55.004(d)(5) — the timely-billing analysis here feeds directly into Entry 4's lien-amount calculation. Computing them in isolation over-states liens. See Entry 4(f).

## ENTRY 10 — Compelled medical examinations (TRCP 204.1 / Sherwin-Williams line)

**Status:** DRAFT — primary text read 2026-07-26; wording sign-off pending; entry status NOT verified.

**Cite:** Tex. R. Civ. P. 204.1; In re Sherwin-Williams Co., 668 S.W.3d 368 (Tex. 2023) [READ 2026-07-26]; In re Auburn Creek Ltd. P'ship, 655 S.W.3d 837 (Tex. 2022) [READ 2026-07-26]; In re H.E.B. Grocery Co., 492 S.W.3d 300 (Tex. 2016) [READ 2026-07-26]; In re Club Car, LLC, No. 02-25-00532-CV, 2025 Tex. App. LEXIS 9102 (Tex. App.—Fort Worth Nov. 25, 2025, orig. proceeding) (mem. op.) [READ].

**(a) Good-cause test.** [READ 2026-07-26 — Sherwin-Williams; confirmed verbatim, third element included; also Club Car, quoting it] Movant must show: (1) examination relevant to a condition in controversy and likely to lead to relevant evidence; (2) reasonable nexus between examination and the condition; (3) desired information not obtainable by less intrusive means.

**(a-1) The nexus element has independent content.** [READ 2026-07-26 — Auburn Creek] *Auburn Creek* does not collapse into *Sherwin-Williams*; it gives the nexus element operative content: on reasonable nexus, the movant must provide **more than conclusory allegations** and must show **more than mere relevance** to the case — there must be **evidence that the requested examination directly relates to the condition in controversy**. Application template: the defense-designated expert, Dr. Gilbert Martinez, testified that neuropsychiatric examinations would assess the claimed injuries — memory impairment, language difficulty, anxiety, depression, processing deficiencies — for each of the plaintiffs (the Pau family), a variety of disorders and symptoms itself listed in Dr. Webb's report served on the plaintiffs' behalf; Martinez proposed a lengthy list of possible tests, many similar to those Webb had performed, with no objection to any particular test; he testified he could not be certain which tests were appropriate until a clinical interview and firsthand behavioural observation, and identified a risk of bias and error if patients knew in advance which tests would be administered. **That evidence established the reasonable nexus.** Plaintiff-side lesson `[JUDGMENT]`: the nexus is satisfied by tying *specific proposed testing* to *specific symptoms in the claimant's own expert's report*; a generic list of tests without that tie-in does not clear the bar — that is the shape of the objection. Scope side-issue, preserved for (d): the *Auburn Creek* parties disputed whether Martinez had sufficiently limited the scope of the examinations, and the trial court denied both motions on that requirement at least in part — scope limitation is live and litigable, Rule 204.1 "reasonable and appropriate terms and conditions" territory, not a formality.

**(a-2) The less-intrusive-means element.** [READ 2026-07-26 — H.E.B.] Facts, because they are the measure of how ordinary a case can be and still compel an exam: the plaintiff (Rodriguez) tripped over a metal plate at a cart corral at a Brownsville H.E.B.; while the suit was pending, a roll of artificial turf carried by a Sam's Club employee fell on his head — an intervening injury. H.E.B.'s retained orthopaedic surgeon Dr. William Swan opined **from the records** that Rodriguez had a pre-existing spinal condition, that the spinal injury was present with or without the fall, and that nothing on the MRI suggested an acute injury; H.E.B. moved for an examination; the trial court denied it; mandamus issued. Relevance: the issues in controversy were the existence, extent, and cause of the injuries, and Swan expected to glean information on those very issues. Nexus: the examination targeted the condition in controversy directly. **Less intrusive means — where the element actually turns:** the claimant's same-information-obtainable-less-intrusively argument failed because the records were voluminous and reflected complex, potentially conflicting diagnoses; the tests already performed did not necessarily provide the same information a physical examination would; Swan explained at deposition that a hands-on doctor is in a better position to evaluate the injuries than a records-review doctor; and requiring Swan to testify at trial without examining Rodriguez places him at a distinct disadvantage, letting the claimant call his credibility into question before the jury. The Court concluded an examination was **crucial to a fair adjudication** and H.E.B. should be permitted to develop its case through its own expert in the same manner as the claimant. Note: the credibility-disadvantage point recurs across these opinions; it is not unique to *H.E.B.*

**(b) "Records review suffices" argument fails.** [READ — Club Car] Serving an expert report on substantial claimed injuries puts them in controversy; defense doctors are not required to rely on plaintiff's doctor's records; requiring the defense expert to testify without examining the plaintiff creates a credibility disadvantage the supreme court has rejected. Conclusory-affidavit attacks on the defense doctors' need-to-examine statements rejected on modest affidavit language.

**(c) Trend.** [READ — Club Car; JUDGMENT] [FLAG — 2026-07-26: trend framing questioned; awaiting ruling, open item V3] Second Court granting mandamus to compel exams in quick succession (Club Car; In re Progressive Cnty. Mut., Nov. 21, 2025). Expect exams to be compelled statewide post-Sherwin-Williams where damages experts are served on TBI/CRPS/neuropsych-probeable injuries.

**(d) Practice hook (case manager, not registry-computed):** On serving a damages expert report in a PI case, fire an IME-anticipation task: client counseling, proposed protocol, terms-and-conditions positions (scope, duration, recording, attendance) — the mandate leaves "reasonable and appropriate terms and conditions" to the trial court, so that is where the fight is. `[JUDGMENT — proposed, unruled, added 2026-07-26]` Cross-cutting pattern across all three IME cases: in each, the movant prevailed because it put on **actual evidence** — expert testimony explaining why the examination was needed and why the records alone would not do; conclusory assertion loses. That is the single most useful line for resisting an examination, and it is what this practice hook should say.

## DRAFT ENTRY — UNADOPTED: service diligence / timely service (H21) — Tex. State Univ. v. Tanner

**Status:** DRAFT — *Tanner* read 2026-07-26. **ADOPTION NOT RULED (open item V6). Not a registry entry until Michael adopts it.**

> **Routing note.** Michael read *Tanner* in full and read the operative passages aloud; he did **not** answer the question "do you adopt *Tanner* as the H21 cite." This draft is placed here provisionally because the registry draft-entry queue lives in this file, but it is **not medical-billing subject matter** — its other candidate home is `docs/specs/trcp-deadline-skeleton-2026-03-01.md`, where H21 originates (that doc's §6 flags service diligence as "case law, no rule cite — registry entry needed," and its §9 now points at this draft). **Michael routes it; do not treat this placement as a decision.**

`[READ — Tex. State Univ. v. Tanner, 689 S.W.3d 292 (Tex. 2024), 2026-07-26]`

**(a) There is no freestanding diligence requirement.** The court of appeals "analyzed the wrong question." There is no freestanding "diligent service requirement" — only a requirement of **timely service**. Diligence in attempting service prevents the running of limitations so long as the plaintiff truly labours to achieve service of process. Where service is diligently effected after limitations has expired, **the date of service relates back to the date of filing**. Diligence for its own sake is immaterial; exercising diligence is merely the means to the end of establishing timely service, **the absence of which can doom a plaintiff's claim**. This reframes H21 substantially: the proposition is **not** "you must be diligent in serving" — it is "service after limitations relates back only if you were diligent."

**(b) The diligence standard.** Whether the plaintiff acted as an **ordinarily prudent person** would have acted under the same or similar circumstances, and was diligent **up to the time the defendant was served**. The inquiry is necessarily fact-intensive, determined by examining the time it took to secure citation, service, or both, and the type of effort — or lack of effort — the plaintiff expended in procuring service.

**(c) The operative teeth.** A lapse of time between service efforts, standing alone, does not prove lack of diligence. But **any delay in service after limitations has run requires an explanation** — and not a conclusory or perfunctory one. The plaintiff's explanation of its service efforts may itself demonstrate lack of due diligence **as a matter of law**, as where one or more lapses between service efforts are **unexplained or patently unreasonable**.

**(d) Against a governmental defendant, untimeliness is jurisdictional.** Where a plaintiff filed within limitations but did not serve a governmental entity until years after limitations expired, and the defendant moved to dismiss for lack of timely service, **a plea to the jurisdiction is a proper vehicle** to challenge the timeliness of service under Tex. Gov't Code §311.034. Remand was required because the lower court had not reached the issue, having concluded untimely service did not present a jurisdictional question a plea to the jurisdiction could resolve; the defendant's alternative summary-judgment motion relied on the same timeliness arguments.

**Build consequence `[JUDGMENT — proposed, unruled]`:** governmental-defendant cases warrant their own escalation profile. The failure mode is **dismissal for want of jurisdiction** — not waivable, and it can surface late — rather than an ordinary limitations fight. And the escalation logic for *every* case follows from (c): once limitations has passed on an unserved defendant, **every gap between service attempts needs a documented, non-conclusory explanation in the file**, because that is precisely what a court will demand. That is a concrete thing the case heartbeat can prompt for, and it is the first heartbeat prompt in the project whose output is *evidence* rather than a reminder.

**Relationship to H24.** H24 (file at least six months before limitations, unconditional buffer) has no cite and no sign-off. *Tanner* supplies its rationale — the buffer exists to keep the case out of the relation-back analysis entirely. That does not adopt H24; it explains it.
